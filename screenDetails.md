Referral maintenance.
Administrators can amend or cancel referrals or move them to another session (even if that exceeds capacity with a client generated warning). They also accept or reject the referrals that are waiting for a decision.

#Login
Authentication will eventually be via a Google id. Initial version will be a dummy signon page with no validation, but this should still generate an expiring security token, with refresh tokens etc.

The sign-on screen and the public referral form carry the Foodbank logo as a banner above their page heading.

#Stock maintenance
There are about 120 stock items, with a maintenance facility to amend them. A weekly stock take is the source of truth: it shows the stock items in shelf order, forty at a time, and saves each page independently. The current value is visible as a reference, but only a count that differs from it is saved; leaving a row unchanged does not overwrite its history. A counter can return to a saved page and amend it again, and only the change from the most recently saved value is sent.
There will also be a list screen with items and current stock, in shelf order, to support the stock take.
Stock is maintained in a certain place in the warehouse. So each stock item has a shelf number. A stock item also has a category, which is compulsory and is used to sort stock-item maintenance and pick-list maintenance alphabetically, followed by item name. It has an optional description, which says what the item actually is where its name alone does not. Stock-item maintenance shows and maintains name, category, description and shelf number. The stock take and the printed picking sheet remain in shelf order. Units and low-stock thresholds are not maintained.

#Picking list
Against a session there will be an option to view picking list. If that picking list has not been created yet, then it will be done at this point. For each referral, the number of adults and children will result via a series of rules into a model picking list.
This picking list can be easily modified (add new items, change quantities). Pick-list maintenance shows every active stock item in category and item-name order, with a blank quantity where the parcel does not contain the item; a retired item remains shown only where that parcel already contains it. It also shows household composition as a compact icon grid: age bands run down the left (crawling baby, child kicking a football, teenager with headphones, adult, older adult with a stick), while columns across the top use the female and male symbols, a neutral person for Non-Binary, and a question mark for Prefer not to say. The numbers are the only visible cell text. Hovering an icon shows what it means — the age band or the gender it stands for — so a team leader who does not recognise a symbol can find out where they are, without a legend taking more room than the grid. It is a convenience for a pointer, not the only way the meaning is available: the labels are read out to anyone using a screen reader whether they hover or not. A checked-by-default checklist, “Show unselected Stock items”, controls whether active items with a blank (zero) quantity are shown. It filters the already-loaded stock catalogue in the client, so checking or unchecking it does not call the database. This generates a printable picking list page per referral, with a referral number and the stock items they require and quantities. The printed sheet remains in shelf order and shows the optional item description under its name when there is one. It carries the same compact household-composition grid.

After picking is complete, modifications can be made to the list.

When a picking list is selected, it must show the referral's answers to the preference questions - the ones on the referral form that are about what the household wants or can eat, such as whether they need flour and whether it should be plain or self raising. These are what guide the person maintaining the list: the model parcel gives the starting point, and the preferences are how it gets adjusted for that household. Without them on the screen the list can only be maintained by guesswork.

This is a screen requirement, not a printing one. What appears on a printed sheet is decided separately, and the reason for referral never appears on either.

#The printed picking sheet
One sheet per parcel, and the whole picking list is printed in one go rather than a sheet at a time.

The pick number goes on the sheet large. It is what matches a sheet to a bag in a hall, and a picker reads it across a table. The referral's own number goes on it too, so a sheet can be traced back to the referral behind it.

The stock items and their quantities are listed in the order the server gives them, which is shelf order, so a picker walks the aisle once. That order is never rearranged on the way to the paper - not alphabetically, not by quantity, not by anything else.

The reason for referral never appears on a printed sheet. Not for an administrator either. Sheets are carried round halls and left on tables.

The referee's name goes on every sheet, so that a volunteer handing a bag over can tell it is the right one. Nothing else about them does unless the parcel is a delivery: then the sheet says DELIVERY, and carries the address, the postcode and the phone number, because the address is the point of that sheet and a driver who cannot find the door needs to ring.

Anything the picker needs in order to make up that particular parcel goes on the sheet prominently, because the picker is the only person in a position to act on it. When a pick list is created, configured referral answers are copied into its Pick-list information as a compact labelled snapshot ; for example, Allergies and Beans. Both the box a team lead edits it in and the block it prints in are headed Information for pickers, naming who it is for. The team lead can amend or clear that information while the session remains open. The printed sheet shows only that saved Pick-list information, never live referral answers or the reason for referral.

#Model parcels and the household grid
The "series of rules" that turns a household into a model picking list is a lookup, not a calculation.

There is a table of model picking lists. Each has a name as its key and its contents as JSON - the stock items and quantities that make up that parcel.

Separately there is a grid of every possible household size. Adults run from 1 to 5 and children from 0 to 5, so there are 30 entries. A household larger than 5 adults counts as 5, and more than 5 children counts as 5. Each grid entry holds just the name of the model picking list to use for that household size, so several household sizes can share one model parcel and changing that parcel updates all of them. The grid is maintained as a whole and updated in one go, so it can be stored as a single row.

A referral must have at least one adult — that is, at least one person aged 12 or over — so every referral maps to a real grid entry.

The model picking lists are not versioned. When a picking list is created for a session, the contents of the relevant model are copied onto each referral's parcel. From then on that parcel holds its own contents and is unaffected by any later change to the model or the grid. The parcel does not record which model it came from - only what is in it.

#Session maintenance
Administrators can create and amend either weekly or ad hoc sessions and update the package limit for each.

Both the screen for one session and the weekly session list offer a way back to the list of scheduled sessions, and saving a session goes back there too. Session maintenance is worked through in passes — open one, change it, return, open the next — and a screen that has to be left by the menu turns each of those into three actions instead of one. The way back is offered even where the session could not be loaded at all, which is the one state where somebody is otherwise stranded on an empty page. Saving a weekly session returns to the weekly list rather than the scheduled one, because that is the list it belongs to and the one the administrator was working through.

Each session also carries whether it takes deliveries at all and, if it does, when they go out. Both are maintained here, on weekly and ad hoc sessions alike, and they are asked in that order because the second only means anything once the first is answered.

Whether the session takes deliveries comes first. Some sessions have nobody to drive, and a household asking for a delivery to one of them is a promise the food bank cannot keep. **A new session takes no deliveries until somebody says otherwise**: a driver is the exception rather than the assumption, and a session wrongly marked as delivering promises a round nobody is doing. Until it is ticked the two delivery times are greyed out and hold nothing, because there is no window to describe.

Once it is ticked, both times are required. Deliveries go out in a window rather than at a moment — the van cannot arrive at a single minute, so what a household is told is a time to be in between — and a session that delivers without saying when leaves the household with nothing to expect and the reminder message with nothing to quote. A window that ends before it starts is refused. Turning deliveries back off clears the window rather than leaving it behind, so a session never carries times it does not use. Nothing is scheduled or routed from any of this: it is only what the household is told to expect, and it is what the referral form reads.

**A session that delivers must say when, and these screens deliberately offer no way to avoid it.** The API will accept a delivering session with no window of its own and treat it as delivering across the session's own hours; the charity does not want that, confirmed on 2026-08-16, because the session's own hours are when the hall is open and not when the van is out — that is the whole reason a delivery window exists. So the fallback is not offered here, and this is a decision rather than an omission: anyone finding the gap between what the API allows and what these screens permit should leave it as it is. A session already in that state still reads correctly wherever it is shown, and can be moved out of it but not back.

A session is named to a reader by its date and its hours, and never by its location. The charity runs from one hall, so printing its name on every row and every dropdown option repeated the same words down the page and pushed the parts that do differ off the edge of a phone. The location is still maintained against the session; it is simply not what identifies one. Where the location used to sit, a session that takes no deliveries reads `Collection Only`, and one that takes them as usual reads nothing at all — collection is the ordinary case and saying so every time would be the same noise again. Should the food bank ever run from more than one place, the location has to come back into that line.

Where a session is offered for choosing — a dropdown, or a list of links — it reads as one line of text. Where it is stated as a description of the session being worked on or planned, it reads as aligned columns: the date, the hours, `Collection Only` or nothing, and how many households are booked. The columns line up down a list so the eye runs along one of them rather than re-finding it on every row, and the empty collection-only cell still holds its place so the count after it stays in line. The list of all sessions an administrator maintains keeps its status as a further column, because a cancelled session that reads like any other is how somebody comes to run a session that is not happening. On a narrow screen the columns stack, where alignment buys nothing and sideways scrolling would cost a volunteer a tap.

#Session processing
Team leaders, and administrators covering for them, have a Run a session option. It lists every open session rather than the calendar-maintenance or referral-maintenance screens. That includes sessions in the past that still need to be completed: outcomes and details are often filled in after the event, so a session that has already happened stays on the list until it is confirmed. A session that has been completed or cancelled is not listed. Selecting a session shows its date, time, location and booking count, followed by its clients.

Opening an editable session creates a pick list for every active client who does not already have one, only when the household grid provides a model parcel for every active client's household size. Generation is all-or-nothing: if the grid is incomplete, no new pick lists are created and the administrator must complete the grid before staff can run the session. Existing parcels are never changed, so a late referral or transfer gets a new pick list without replacing a picker's adjustments. The client list says whether pick lists have been generated; staff select each client in turn to check and update their parcel. If late parcels are created after the list was printed, the screen says that the list must be printed again.

When a pick list is created, the client applies its maintained preference-rule configuration to the referral answers and current active stock catalogue, then sends the resolved item ids with the creation request. Rules run in configured order; a rule which handles a selected preference answer consumes that answer so a later, broader rule for the same preference handles only the remaining answers. Positive quantities from separately handled answers add together. A rule can select an item at a fixed quantity, or mark it as needing attention where the team lead must decide the quantity; attention overrides any positive quantity for that item. A line needing attention is shown as such rather than as a negative number. It must be given a positive quantity or removed before that parcel can be reviewed, so it cannot be printed or issued. Administrators have a Preference rule check screen which validates the configured question keys, choice values and stock names against the current environment.

Once all pick lists have been generated and every parcel for a current client has been reviewed there is an option to print all of them. Printing is unavailable until then, so a parcel still needing a team leader's decision cannot reach paper. A parcel retained for a cancelled referral is not a client: it is absent from the session client list, direct client workspace, print gate and sheets, completion check, and SMS conversations. The session client list keeps each pick number with the name, followed by a status: Pending Review, Pick List reviewed, Attended/Delivered, or No Show/Not in. A pending client has a Review Pick list link. Once reviewed, Attended/Delivered and No Show/Not in become clickable. A Complete Session button sits above the list and is available only after every client has an outcome. Selecting a client opens a separate client workspace, so the session client list does not compete for screen space. Its heading shows the pick number and client name. The two operational counts are not shown to a team leader anywhere: they are the grid's axes rather than a description of the people the bag is for, and the household composition grid says that properly. That heading is the first thing on the screen and the only place the pick number appears there: it is not repeated again over the pick list below, where it says nothing the heading has not already said. The client workspace has prominent Save and Mark Pick List reviewed buttons above the Information for pickers box, so the actions a team lead reaches for repeatedly come before the note they rarely retype. Attendance, Complete Session and printing all stay on the client list — printing commits every sheet for the session, and the workspace is about one household. The pick list and the client’s preferences are shown in separate, independently scrollable panels, each taking at least one third of the screen. Category change headers are smaller and lighter than stock-item names. Preferences use the referral field key and the answer in aligned columns; the full question is not repeated. Staff can alter several quantities and add items while considering the preferences, then save all those changes with one Save action. If they leave the client workspace while changes have not been saved, the system asks whether to save first. To confirm attendance (or delivery to) a client (or to confirm non attendance - one or the other must be done)
After the session, each referee is marked as attended or did not show. If they attended, the stock is updated. Otherwise the parcel will be unpacked. Until the session is confirmed, either outcome can be changed to correct a mis-tap; changing attended to no show puts that parcel's stock back, and changing it back to attended takes it again.
Only when all clients have been confirmed as attended or no show (or delivered/not delivered) can the session be confirmed and closed. A closed session and its pick list cannot be changed.

#Menus
Every signed-in screen has a Menu button which opens a popup that remains within the viewport and scrolls if necessary. If the id is an admin account then the popup is grouped as Referrals (Run a session, Check referrals, Send to Sheets), Stock (Stock, Stock Take, Stock Items, Model Parcels, Parcel Grid), Sessions (Manage Sessions, Weekly sessions), Master Data (Reasons for Crisis, Users, Approved referrers), then SMS Messages and Fuel. If a team leader, the same popup presents their permitted options as one list: Run a session, Stock and Stock Take. Calendar maintenance and the general referral list are not team lead tasks.

#Valid referrers
A table of valid referrers is maintained. These are either full email addresses or any email address for a given domain (*@organisation.com). Only administrators can maintain these

#Referrals
The flow for referrals is that the referrer will put in their email address. This is checked against the charity's list, and either way they are then asked for information about the client. Some information is fixed - the referrer's own name and organisation, the client's first name, surname, date of birth, address, postcode and phone number, the session, the household composition, the main cause of crisis, whether a delivery is needed and whether they need help with fuel - but some is subject to change. Household composition is one grid with age ranges down the left (0-4, 5-11, 12-17, 18 to State Pension age, State Pension age or over) and Female, Male, Non-Binary and Prefer not to say across the top. Empty cells are shown blank and only non-zero counts are retained in the `Household Components` JSON answer. The client derives the fixed operational fields from the grid, and they are sizing figures rather than a description of the household: an adult is anyone aged 12 or over, a child is anyone aged 5 to 11, and the under-fives count towards neither. A teenager eats an adult's share and an infant is fed from elsewhere, so those two counts are what the household grid and the preference rules run on. The grid itself is retained for reporting, and it is the grid — never the two counts — that describes who lives in the household. A referral must include at least one person aged 12 or over. The confirmation shown back to a referrer is the exception: it counts the household the way the two words are normally used, adults being everyone 18 or over and children everyone under 18, so a referrer can recognise the household they have just entered. Delivery is derived from the Collection method answer: `Delivery Requested` makes the delivery flag true and every other collection method makes it false. A `No Answer` questionnaire row is display-only text and has no response or JSON key. A question allowing at most one choice is a dropdown; a required question with no default begins with `-- choose --`, and no separate choice instruction is shown. Questions allowing more than one choice remain checkboxes with their instruction. The actual questions to be asked and the validation for them will be in a json config file as part of the client. This is replacing a google form, so basic validation of drop downs, radio buttons, free form text, number validation etc.
This is then submitted to the server.
The session that the client will attend will be from a drop down of the available sessions (non full) for the net 2 weeks.

The questions come from the charity's live google form, which has now been reviewed and supplied. The fixed fields above are settled and so are the additional questions; what remains open is the wording of some option lists, and those are listed as open questions rather than guessed at on screen.

Asking for a delivery takes two confirmations together: that the client meets the criteria for delivery, and that the client will be at home for the delivery time. Both are ticked or the form does not move on, because either alone is not what is being asked. Directly above them the form states the chosen session's delivery window and the date it falls on, so the second confirmation is about a real time a referrer can put to a household rather than an abstraction. That line carries a bold label, "Delivery Time:", with the window itself in ordinary text after it, and it is set at the same size and contrast as the questions around it rather than as small print: it is the one line the confirmation beneath it asks a referrer to agree to, and written as a plain sentence it was read straight past. That line is written into the questionnaire configuration as a variable the chosen session fills in, not as a question hard-coded into the screen, so it stays maintained the same way every other question is. It follows that the session is asked before the collection method: it is the answer both the line and the confirmation depend on, and until a session is chosen the line and the confirmation are simply not shown.

Where the chosen session takes no deliveries, that line says so instead of quoting a window the food bank cannot keep. It does not stop the referral. Delivery stays on offer and the form still submits, which leaves the referrer confirming in as many words that the household will be at home for a session with no deliveries — visible nonsense, which an administrator picks up when they read the referral. That is deliberate: refusing it would tell the referrer nothing about why the option had gone, and the charity would rather have the referral and the conversation than neither.

Two kinds of question live in that configuration and it is worth keeping them apart. Questions about the person are what the referral is; preference questions - do they need flour, plain or self raising or none - are what the picking list is adjusted from. It is the second kind the picking list screen has to show.

The form is asked a page at a time, with a title on each page, because it is long and a referrer fills it in on a phone. Moving on is refused while something mandatory on the page in front of them is missing, and nothing on a later page is complained about before they have got there. How far through they are is always on the screen.

Where a question allows no answer at all, the screen offers "None" as a choice of its own and starts there unless the form says otherwise. None means none: choosing it clears everything else, choosing anything else clears it, and it is not recorded as an answer. Where a question allows several answers, the screen says how many may be chosen and stops accepting more once that many are.

A question that only applies because of an earlier answer is greyed out until that answer is given, and anything already typed into it is forgotten when it greys out again. The referral must never carry an answer to a question that is not on the screen.

The client's postcode is stored in capitals with a single space, however it was typed, because it is searched on and one household spelled three ways matches nothing.

The referrer's email address is checked as they type it, not after they have filled the form in. Somebody the charity does not recognise is told so before they have typed a household's details, not after - but they are not turned away. Their referral is taken and held for an administrator to accept or reject, and the screen says that is what will happen. When the address is recognised, the organisation it belongs to is already known and the form fills that in for them rather than asking - into an empty box only, never over an organisation the referrer has chosen for themselves. When it is not, the form asks which organisation they are from, offering the ones the charity already knows and letting them type one that is not there.

Recognising somebody is said as soon as it is known, but not recognising somebody is only said once they have finished with the address and moved on. Half of a real address looks like a whole one - pete@guildford.gov on the way to pete@guildford.gov.uk - and the charity does not tell a referrer it has never heard of them over an address they are still typing. Editing the address again takes the statement back until they have finished with it once more.

A household larger than the grid allows is not an error and is not shown as one. Anything over five adults or five children counts as five, so a household of nine receives the same parcel as five. If the screen says anything about it at all, it says it as information.

#After a referral is submitted
The referrer cannot change or withdraw a referral once it is submitted. There is no self-service route at all, and there is no window in which there is one.

Instead the screen confirms what was sent, showing back every answer that had to be given, because that is the referrer's only chance to notice that a surname or a session date is wrong. The session and the reason for referral are shown as the words the referrer chose them by - the session as its date, time and location, the reason as its wording - and never as the internal identifiers they are submitted as, because a referrer cannot check an identifier and checking is the only thing this screen is for. A referral that is waiting to be accepted says so plainly, so that nobody leaves believing a household is booked in when it is not.

To change anything after that the referrer telephones the food bank and an administrator makes the change. The screen says so plainly. This is a normal ending, not a fault, and it is not apologised for.

#Referrals awaiting a decision
A referral from an email address the charity does not recognise is held until an administrator accepts or rejects it. It holds its place on the session from the moment it is sent: it counts against capacity, a parcel is picked for it, and it appears on the pick list and the printed sheets like any other. Unless an administrator rejects it, the household is presumed to be a genuine client.

What the referrer is told is deliberately more cautious than that, and the two must not be reconciled. The confirmation screen tells the referrer the household is not booked in yet and that nobody should turn up until the food bank confirms it, because the referral genuinely may still be rejected and a household turned away at the door is the worst outcome there is. The food bank contacts the household one way or the other. But contact is not always possible, and a household who never got the message may turn up anyway — so the team leader has their parcel ready and serves them. One statement is advice to a referrer, the other is what the session actually does; neither is a mistake in the other's terms.

Two quite different things happen to a referral, and the screens must never let them read as one. The first is a decision about the **referrer**: an unrecognised address means somebody must say whether the charity accepts referrals from them at all, and only some referrals need it. The second is an administrator **reading the referral through**, which every referral waits for whoever sent it. So a referral held on an unrecognised referrer is shown as `Approve referrer`, one that is booked but not yet read through as `Pending review`, and one that has been read as `Reviewed`. The first of those must not be worded with the word review: beside `Reviewed` it reads as the same job at an earlier stage, and it is not — a referral can be approved and still unread, and read without ever having needed approving. A status is shown whole on one line wherever it appears, rather than wrapping within its own outline.

The referral list shows the ones waiting first, and marks each Approve referrer line as needing attention, because a household waiting on a decision is more urgent than one already booked in.

Accepting or rejecting one takes a single line of comment - why it was let through, or why it was not. There is one comment on a referral and a later review replaces it. It is not timestamped; the referral already records when it was submitted, and that is the date anybody asks about. Only administrators see it, because it can name a referrer or record a suspicion.

When an administrator opens a referral to review it, the screen shows how many earlier referrals appear to be for the same household in the last twelve months and the date of the most recent matching session. The administrator can then choose to show the previous referrals. Each one shows its household details, session date, whether the household attended, did not attend, or is still booked, and which of date of birth, postcode and phone number matched. This information helps the administrator make a judgement; it never blocks accepting, rejecting or reading a referral. The duplicate-referrals section has an `Exclude postcode matches` checkbox, initially unticked. Ticking it recalculates both its count and its list using date of birth and phone number only, so a shared hostel or refuge postcode does not dominate the result. Team leaders do not see the count or previous referrals.

Administrators have a separate referral-search screen, reached from the referrals menu. It searches by date of birth, postcode or phone number; date of birth is the usual search, and postcode and phone number are alternatives. The three read in that order on one line, labelled `DoB`, `or Postcode` and `or Phone number`, so an administrator can see at a glance that the second and third are the fallbacks when a date of birth is not to hand. When more than one is supplied, a referral matching any one is shown, and the screen does not say so in words — the labels carry it. An optional start-of-surname filter, on a line of its own below the identifiers and used alongside at least one of them, narrows the result set to distinguish clients sharing a date of birth. Each result uses two compact lines: session date, referral status, the household name as surname then first name, postcode, phone number and referrer organisation on the first line; then the primary cause, secondary cause, additional crisis information and the administrator notes, separated by `/` without headings, on the second. The notes belong on that line because the office writes there what it learned from ringing the household, and an administrator taking a call needs it at the same moment they need the causes, without opening each referral in turn. The result table can scroll horizontally instead of making its first-line columns wider. Opening a result and coming back is the ordinary way this screen is used — an administrator on the phone works down a list of possible matches — so the referral opened from a result offers a way back to the search results, and returning shows the same search and the same list rather than an empty form. The list must be current: where the referral was changed while it was open — amended, moved, cancelled, accepted, rejected or marked reviewed — the search runs again on return, so an administrator reading the row back to a household on the phone is never reading what it said before the change they just made. That way back is offered only when the search is where the referral was opened from, and the search itself never appears in a web address. The client takes the additional and secondary information from the returned dynamic answers according to the current referral-form configuration; the server does not know what those answer keys mean. It is an administrator-only search because these identifiers and its results are personal information.

Every referral also waits for an administrator to read it through. A referral from a recognised address starts Pending review; once it is read, it becomes Reviewed without changing its booking or parcel. The detail screen offers an administrator a Mark reviewed button once, on the same line as cancelling and moving, while the referral is Pending review — the three are what an administrator does with a referral they have just read, and a button repeated at each end of the page read as two different actions. The referral list highlights Pending review rows so that the referrals still to be read stand out from Reviewed ones.

The referral detail screen shows its header information, then the previous possible referrals, then the approval/rejection and cancellation controls. Its header shows the referral's status and, beside it, what became of the household: Attended/Delivered, No Show/Not in, or still booked. The status alone does not say whether a household that held a place actually came, and that is the first thing an administrator wants when the household is on the phone about a session they missed. What became of the household is shown only where it says something the status does not: a cancelled or rejected referral shows its status alone, because nothing happened on the day and reading that the household is still booked beside the words that say they are not is worse than not being told. An administrator sees the administrator notes directly under the household name and can change just those notes in a small Save/Cancel dialog; the section, its edit control and the dialog all call the field administrator notes, because that is what the office calls what it writes there. The other fixed fields are read-only until an administrator chooses Edit, which expands to the current pages of the referral form. Choosing a page opens the same questions and controls that the referrer saw, with the referral's complete stored answers available for conditional questions. Saving validates and changes that page, preserving every answer from every other page and every stored answer whose key is no longer on the current form; cancelling changes nothing. The administrator’s referral view shows household composition only in the same compact icon grid used for pick lists, and shows every other answer that was actually given, including a stored key no longer on the form, but not a skipped question. It also shows the two operational counts, labelled `Adults (>11)` and `Children (5-11)` so that the ages they cover are on the screen beside them: an administrator is the one person who corrects those numbers, and bare `Adults` would be read as everyone over 18 and corrected to the wrong figure. A team leader is not shown them at all and sees the composition grid in their place. The referral list is the exception and keeps the plain words `adults` and `children`, where the figures are a rough sense of household size rather than something anyone acts on. Marking the referral reviewed, cancelling it and moving it to another session are buttons side by side on one line, because they are what an administrator does having just read the referral through — and cancelling and moving are the two answers to the same phone call, that the household cannot come to this session. Neither acts on the spot: cancelling asks for confirmation and an optional reason, and moving asks which session to move to, warning where the chosen session is already full without refusing the move. The other answers from the referral form come last. The reason-for-referral and session-to-move controls are large enough to read and operate comfortably.

Approving a referral whose referrer the charity does not recognise asks for no reason at all — it is the ordinary outcome, and typing something to explain it is work for nothing. Rejecting one does ask, in the rejection dialog and optionally, because there is no review history and that one line is the whole answer to "why was this household turned away?" months later.

#Copying a referral
A household that did not turn up, or whose referral was cancelled or rejected, often rings the food bank afterwards, and the charity gives them another chance. The original referral is the record of what actually happened and is never reopened or altered — the no show stays where it is — so a second chance is a new referral copied from it. The referral detail screen offers an administrator Copy this referral, and offers it only where the referral can no longer be completed: where the household was recorded as No Show/Not in, or where the referral was cancelled or rejected. A referral still on its way to being completed is not copied; moving it is the answer to that phone call, and the two must not be offered as alternatives for the same referral.

The copy carries every detail of the original — the household, its answers, the reason for referral and the referrer — except its status and its session. The administrator chooses the session it goes on from the same picker offered when moving a referral, and is warned but not refused where the chosen session is already full. Unlike a move, the picker offers the session the original is already on: a household who cancelled and rang back can be copied onto the very session they cancelled from, and that is an ordinary phone call rather than an edge case. The copy is approved and read without being asked about either: the administrator making it has just decided that this household should come, so there is nothing left for a later administrator to accept or to read through. Its administrator notes are set to `Copied from referral dated ...`, the date the original referral was submitted, and they replace any notes the original carried rather than being added to them. Once the copy is made the screen shows the new referral, because that is the one the administrator is now working with; the original is left as it stands. Nothing limits an administrator to one copy — a referral that has already been copied can be copied again, and the administrator making it is trusted to know why.

#Session processing screens
A session cannot be closed while anybody on it is still unmarked. When the screen refuses to close a session it names the pick numbers still waiting, because what the team leader needs to know is who is missing.

Marking a referee attended or a delivery delivered is reversible until the session is confirmed, so it is sent directly rather than confirmed. After confirmation no outcome or pick list can be changed.

From the selected Run a session screen, a team leader or administrator can open a referral-details screen for that session. It lists each household on the session with the client name, address, postcode and phone number, and the referrer's name and phone number. It contains no reason for referral, date of birth, answers, review comment or parcel contents. The screen can be printed when the information is needed on paper.

#Listener sheet
From a Run a session screen, a team leader or administrator can open and print one listener sheet for that session. Like Print all pick lists, the link is on the selected-session screen, but unlike it the listener sheet is available regardless of whether pick lists have been created or reviewed. It is a printed page for the dedicated trained volunteers who discuss the household's situation with clients and offer guidance where appropriate. It includes every non-delivery household except cancelled and rejected referrals. It shows every referral-form column marked for the listener sheet, in the order of the referral form; the marker is the single authority for what a listener sees, and the sheet carries no second fixed list of its own. Where a marked detail is one the listener sheet does not carry, that column is left out altogether rather than standing empty on every sheet. What the charity marks today is the household name, the reason for crisis, the additional information about it, the secondary cause and the fuel-help flag. Every column reads as words a listener can say out loud: the secondary cause is chosen from the same maintained list as the main one and is stored as a reference to it, so the sheet looks the reason up and prints what it is called, never the reference. A reason retired since the referral was made cannot be looked up, and the column says so plainly rather than printing a reference or claiming the question went unanswered; the sheet is not printed at all while the list is unavailable, because a page of references is worse than a page a team leader can fetch again. It never contains an address, postcode, phone number, referrer information, date of birth or parcel contents, and no unmarked answer reaches it. It is the only printed document on which a team leader may see the reason for referral, and is for the selected listeners only.

#Fuel help list
A fuel administrator signs into one screen: the fuel help list. Administrators can also open it; team leaders cannot. It is a plain table intended to be copied into Excel, with one row for every referral returned by the server. It shows the parcel session date and every answerable referral-form column marked for the fuel team, in the order of the referral form. The marker is the single authority for which client details and answers may appear: the screen does not carry a second fixed list of columns or show any unmarked answer. Where a marked client detail is one the fuel list does not carry, that column is left out altogether rather than standing empty on every row, because a column that never has a value is worse than no column in a table whose purpose is to be pasted into a spreadsheet. The client's date of birth is shown, because some of the organisations the fuel team hands a household on to will not act without an age. It is shown as a date and never as an age, since an age is right on the day it is written down and wrong a year later. Whether the household needs help with energy costs is not a column: every household on this list does, or it would not be listed. Its introductory text does not tell the user how to interpret the permission answer.

#Spreadsheet extract
An administrator can start an extract of confirmed, unextracted sessions to the food bank's fixed Google spreadsheet. Starting says that it might take some time and asks whether to continue. Only after they agree does the screen ask that administrator for Google Sheets permission; the Google access token is kept only while the extract is running and is never stored by the food bank system.

The extract finds one completed session at a time, writes every referral on it to the spreadsheet, marks that session extracted only after the write succeeds, and then continues. It stops after twenty sessions and asks the administrator whether to continue with another batch. Progress is shown while the screen remains open. If the browser is closed, reloaded or the machine sleeps, the extract stops and the administrator starts another batch later.

The spreadsheet is a fixed document configured separately for each deployment. Its archive has a hidden first row of immutable column keys and a visible second row of headings which the charity may edit. The extract uses only the hidden keys to place data, preserving the visible headings. Its hidden metadata sheet holds the mapping between an answer key and its column. The extract reads that mapping at the start, puts every answer for the same key into that key's one column, and when it adds a new answer key writes that key to both archive header rows and the metadata sheet; the visible heading starts as the key and may then be changed. The extract contains no names, addresses, email addresses or telephone numbers. Its only personal-information columns are postcode and date of birth. Sending is a deliberate action because it sends household details outside the system. A successful spreadsheet write followed by a failed mark can result in duplicate rows; every row carries the referral id so that a duplicate can be found and removed. The system never retries a timed-out spreadsheet write automatically.

#SMS reminders and replies
The Run a session screen lets a team leader or administrator send SMS reminders and shows the total number of unread incoming messages prominently. A reminder goes to every household holding a place, including one pending review, but never to a cancelled or rejected referral. The person running the session can press Send SMS reminders again: newly referred households and earlier failures are tried, while households already reminded are not texted twice. The result states how many were sent, failed and already sent, with failures prominent.

Each household on the session has an expandable message conversation, marked out when it has unread incoming messages. Opening it marks incoming messages read and shows the complete conversation in order. A team leader or administrator can reply from there. The reply control warns that a message must not contain a name, address or anything identifying the household. SMS conversations are never printed or exported.

Administrators also have an Unmatched SMS replies screen for messages with no upcoming session. This is the only SMS screen that shows a phone number, because it is needed to ring the sender back. Messages are only a thirty-day operational record, not a permanent household history.
